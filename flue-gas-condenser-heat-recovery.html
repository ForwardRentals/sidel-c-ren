The basics

What is a flue gas condenser?

A flue gas condenser is a heat exchanger that cools boiler or furnace exhaust below its water dew point, about 135°F (57°C) for natural gas. When the water vapour in the exhaust condenses, it gives back the latent heat it took to boil it. A conventional economizer never reaches that heat, because it is designed to stop at around 250°F so nothing condenses.

That hidden heat is substantial. Burning natural gas produces roughly 10 to 12 gallons of water for every 1,000 cubic feet of gas, and it all leaves the stack as vapour. In one U.S. Department of Energy example, the latent heat in that vapour was about 9 percent of the boiler's firing rate. A flue gas condenser goes after that heat, plus the sensible heat in the hot gas, plus the water itself. See how much heat your boiler sends up the stack.

  • 1. Hot exhaust entersFlue gas from the boiler or furnace, cleaned of particulate upstream by the RPC on dirtier fuels.
  • 2. Heat moves to waterThe gas gives up sensible heat to a cooler water or process loop through corrosion-resistant surfaces.
  • 3. Vapour condensesBelow the dew point, water vapour condenses and releases its latent heat into the same loop.
  • 4. Heat and water reusedWarm water returns to the process. Condensate drains off for neutralization and reuse.

Is it a fit?

Where a flue gas condenser pays off.

The single deciding question is where the recovered heat goes. A condenser can only condense water if something cold is on the other side. These are the conditions that make a project worth a closer look:

  • Long firing hoursBoilers or furnaces that run most of the year, not a few peak weeks.
  • A cold water sinkMakeup water, wash-down, process water or a low-temperature heating loop. Common in dairy and food plants.
  • High stack temperaturesExhaust leaving well above the steam or water temperature it produced.
  • Water costs or limitsSites paying a lot for water, or short of it, gain a second benefit from the condensate.
  • Mismatched timingIf heat is recovered when the plant can't use it, Novacab TESS stores it until it can.
  • Decarbonization targetsEvery unit of fuel not burned is CO₂ not emitted, with no change to the boiler itself.

Flue gas condenser FAQ

Common questions.

What is a flue gas condenser?

A flue gas condenser is a heat exchanger that cools boiler or furnace exhaust below its water dew point, about 135 degrees F for natural gas, so the water vapour in the gas condenses. That recovers both the sensible heat in the hot gas and the latent heat in the vapour, plus liquid water, before the exhaust leaves the stack.

How does a flue gas condenser work?

Hot exhaust passes over corrosion-resistant surfaces carrying cooler water. The gas gives up its heat to the water, and once it drops below the dew point the water vapour condenses and releases its latent heat into the same loop. The warmed water goes back to the process, and the condensate drains away for treatment and reuse.

What is the difference between a flue gas condenser and an economizer?

A standard economizer heats boiler feedwater and keeps the exhaust above roughly 250 degrees F so nothing condenses. A flue gas condenser, also called a condensing economizer, deliberately goes below the dew point to recover the latent heat as well. That needs corrosion-resistant materials and a cold water stream to absorb the heat.

How much energy can a flue gas condenser recover?

It depends on the fuel, the stack temperature and how cold the receiving water is. As a reference point, the U.S. Department of Energy estimates boiler efficiency rises about 1 percent for every 40 degrees F drop in flue gas temperature, and in one DOE example the latent heat alone was about 9 percent of a natural gas boiler's firing rate. Sidel CREN confirms site-specific numbers with a metered baseline before quoting anything.

Can a flue gas condenser be retrofitted to an existing boiler?

Yes. The Sidel SRU is designed to retrofit onto existing boilers and furnaces as well as new installations. The key questions are space at the stack, a suitable cold water or process loop to take the heat, and a drain for the condensate.
